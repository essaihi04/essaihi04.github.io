The Soda That Beat Coca-Cola: the one-page guide

⬇ Download the PDF

Three local sodas beat Coca-Cola at home. Coke answered in three different ways. Figures below are as reported by the sources listed at the end; where sources disagree, we say so.

The deal sheet

Inca Kola (Peru): Coke partnered

  • 1910: the Lindley family opens a bottler in Lima's Rímac district.
  • 1935: Inca Kola launches for Lima's 400th anniversary.
  • 1970: 38% of the Peruvian market.
  • 1995: Inca Kola 32.9% vs Coca-Cola 32%.
  • 1999: Coca-Cola takes half of Inca Kola Perú and a fifth of Lindley. Price never officially disclosed (reports range from about $200M to $300M). Coca-Cola owns the trademark outside Peru.
  • 2005: Lindley buys two thirds of Embotelladora Latinoamericana for $215M.

Thums Up (India): Coke bought it

  • 1977: Coca-Cola leaves India rather than reveal its formula and sell 60% to an Indian partner.
  • Ramesh Chauhan (Parle) creates Thums Up.
  • 1993: Coke buys Thums Up, Gold Spot and Limca for about $60M (most sources). Thums Up held about 85% of India's cola market (widely repeated, secondary sources).
  • 1997: relaunched by Coca-Cola.
  • 2021: a billion-dollar brand in India.

Irn-Bru (Scotland): Coke never bought it

  • 1889: "Ironbrew" in New York; Barr's sells Iron Brew by 1899; official launch 1901.
  • 1948: sold as Irn-Bru.
  • Top-selling soft drink in Scotland for over a century; by 2003 sales of the two were roughly equal; third best-selling soft drink in the UK.
  • A.G. Barr is still independent and listed in London.
  • 2018: sugar cut to under 5g per 100ml ahead of the UK sugar tax.

Three things to notice

  1. In all three cases the local brand won on local taste and local identity, not on a bigger budget.
  2. The global giant's move depended on the situation: a stake when the local bottler was struggling (Peru), an outright purchase when the local brand dominated (India).
  3. Staying independent is possible: Irn-Bru kept its own company, its own formula debates and its own listing.

Two traps when reading brand stories

  1. Market share is not sales growth. Shares quoted for 1970, 1993 and 1995 are snapshots, not trends.
  2. "Reported" is not "confirmed". Prices and shares of private deals are often secondary-source numbers. Check the original filings or press when it matters.

Glossary

  • Market share: the part of all sales in a category that one brand makes.
  • Trademark: the legal right to a brand name and logo.
  • Bottler / franchise: a company licensed to make and sell a drink in a territory.
  • Joint venture: two companies sharing ownership of a business.
  • Acquisition: one company buying another or its brands.
