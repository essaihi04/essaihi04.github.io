Breathometer: the Deal Sheet

⬇ Download the PDF

Case: The Shark Tank startup whose smartphone breathalyzer ended in an FTC settlement.

*Facts only, from the FTC and press coverage. Not investment advice.*

The numbers

| Item | Fact | Source |

|---|---|---|

| Founder | Charles Michael Yim, company founded 2012 | Wikipedia (Breathometer) |

| Indiegogo (2013) | Goal $25,000; raised $138,437 | Fortune, Jan 2017 |

| Shark Tank ask (Season 5, 2013) | $250,000 for 10% = $2.5M valuation | Wikipedia; press |

| Reported deal | $1,000,000 for 30% from all five sharks = about $3.33M valuation | Wikipedia; press |

What the FTC said

  • Marketing claimed "police grade precision" and "government-lab grade testing", with readings from 0 to .25 BAC.
  • Many accuracy tests reportedly covered only up to .02% BAC. The legal driving limit in most US states is .08%.
  • Late 2014: the company learned Breeze readings showed a "downward drift" (users had a higher BAC than displayed).
  • Early 2015: humidity and temperature affected results; sensors deteriorated over time.
  • Q3 2015: retailers told the Breeze would stop selling, with no reason given. Feb 2016: still sold at national retailers.
  • May 2016: retailer letters. June 2016: owner emails. October 6, 2016: breathalyzer feature disabled in the app.

3 things the case shows

  1. Test a claim at the level where the decision is made (.08, not .02).
  2. Tell customers the moment you learn of a safety problem.
  3. Keep written proof for every advertising claim.

2 mistakes it shows

  1. Advertising "police grade" accuracy without evidence to match.
  2. Letting a famous deal stand in for proof that a product works.

Glossary

  • Valuation: what a whole company is worth: amount divided by share ($250,000 / 10% = $2.5M).
  • BAC: blood alcohol content; the share of alcohol in the blood.
  • Substantiation: the proof you must hold before you make an advertising claim.
